## Independent assignment

Train a neural-network controller to move the pendulum toward **0.6 radians** and hold it there. In M2, you adjusted length and damping to match measured motion. In M3, length and damping stay fixed while you adjust network weights and biases to choose useful motor torques.

Implement `train_policy` in `policy.py`. Choose your own code organization and debugging experiments. The assistant reviews saved attempts and offers hints first; implementation code is provided only when requested.

## First decision

## What you have

- `make_policy()` supplies a `2 → 32 → 1` network. Keep this architecture for the baseline.
- `step(th, om, u, L, b)` advances your differentiable simulator.
- `TARGET = 0.6` and `UMAX = 10.0` are already defined.
- `train_policy(L, b, iters=300, lr=0.01, seed=1)` already sets the random seed and creates the network.

## Inputs, outputs, and objective

For each simulated pendulum, give the network **two features**: angle error `th - TARGET` and angular velocity `om`, in that order. For n trajectories, the network input is `(n, 2)`, and its raw output is `(n, 1)`.

Convert raw output into torque using **UMAX × tanh(raw output)**. Torque passed to `step` must have shape `(n,)`, so remove only the final singleton dimension. Each time step needs a fresh action from the updated state: this is feedback control, unlike M2's prerecorded torque schedule.

Minimize this cost averaged across trajectories and simulated time:

```text
mean((angle - TARGET)²) + 0.01 × mean(angular_velocity²)
```

The first term rewards reaching the target; the second discourages fast motion. There are no correct-torque labels. Physics and the objective tell training which actions improve the outcome.

## Requirements

1. Preserve the `train_policy` interface and honor its iteration, learning-rate, and seed arguments. Return the trained policy module, compatible with `evaluate.py`.
2. Use Adam to train the policy parameters. Treat the supplied L and b as fixed physical values; do not optimize them.
3. Use a baseline batch of **32 trajectories**, starting angles sampled uniformly from **[-0.1, 0.1] radians**, and zero starting velocities. Sample new starting angles for each training update.
4. Simulate **150 steps per update**. Choose torque from the current state at every step and average the stated cost over all 150 resulting states and all trajectories.
5. Preserve gradients through the policy, torque transformation, and simulator during training. Use one optimizer update for each full 150-step training batch.
6. Return the original policy network with raw output. Apply the outer torque scaling during simulation, not as an extra layer in the returned network; the evaluation caller handles torque conversion too.
7. Train two policies: one with nominal L = 1.0, b = 0.1, and one with your calibrated physical values. Use the same settings and seed for the comparison. Keep parameter selection outside `train_policy`.
8. Record training costs for both runs without changing the return contract; choose a suitable logging or artifact approach. In WORKLOG.md, record settings, observed results, and explain how the network learns without target torques.

## Success checks

- Network inputs have shape `(n, 2)`, raw outputs `(n, 1)`, and torques `(n,)`; torques remain within [-10, 10].
- Policy parameters receive finite gradients from the trajectory cost and change during training.
- Costs and simulated states stay finite. Cost need not decrease at every update because training states are resampled.
- Compare each policy before and after training on the **same fixed starting angles**: `[-0.1, -0.05, 0.0, 0.05, 0.1]`, with zero velocities and the same 150-step horizon. Aim for lower objective after training and final mean absolute error below **0.1 radians** in its own training simulator; if it misses this target, investigate rather than mark it complete.
